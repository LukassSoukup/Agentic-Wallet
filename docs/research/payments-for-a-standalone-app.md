# Payments for a standalone EU app: virtual cards or scoped tokens

> Research of 2026-11-19 (T-042), compiled from public sources by research agents; claims carry their links. The decision it fed is [ADR-0010](../adr/ADR-0010-standalone-app-and-provider-payment-tokens.md): the user rejected every model that makes the user open and fund an account at an issuer, and chose provider tokens on the user's stored card. Facts about preview products move; re-check them in T-005.

> **Correction (2026-11-19):** Stripe's SPT country list is 32 European countries plus Canada and the US (34 codes); where this report counts it otherwise, this line wins.


**Partly, but not with a one-time virtual card, and not for most EU merchants in November 2026.** A standalone EU consumer app can stay unlicensed, hold no funds and never touch card numbers if it mints a **merchant-, amount- and expiry-scoped payment token** after its own APPROVE decision. Today the only documented way to do that for EU consumers is **Stripe Shared Payment Tokens (SPT)**. SPT is listed for 32 European countries, is funded by the user's own card or wallet, and keeps PANs inside Stripe's iframe. It is still on preview terms, though, and only Stripe merchants that integrated SPT acceptance can redeem it. One-time virtual cards fail the brief for a different reason. Issuers exist: Swan, a French e-money institution (EMI), issues single-use cards to EEA consumers under its own licence. But a plain card number only pays a merchant if someone types it into a checkout or forwards it through an API. The first is the form filling the product forbids, and the second puts the PAN in the app's hands. Google's AP2/UCP stack is the most promising merchant-side protocol, but Google Pay is the only credential provider on Google's own surfaces, and Google has announced no continental-EU launch. The network-level answers (Mastercard Agent Pay, Visa Intelligent Commerce) are live in Europe only as controlled pilots run through banks and enablers. **Recommendation:** design the approval engine around a "scoped credential minted after APPROVE" port. Start with SPT for the narrow set of EU merchants that accept it. Pursue a network-token enabler for reach. Accept, as a product fact, that checkout without form filling reaches only a small, protocol-enabled slice of EU merchants until UCP and agentic tokens mature.

## The brief's three constraints rule out the plain virtual card

The product imposes three hard constraints: the app never holds a licence, never holds customer funds and never sees a card number. Add the ban on browser automation and form filling, and the shape of the answer follows. A one-time virtual card is a 16-digit number with an expiry date and a CVC. A merchant accepts it through a card form or, rarely, a server-side API that takes raw card data. The research found **no general, compliant way for a third-party agent to pay an arbitrary EU merchant server-side with a plain virtual card number**. UCP's DIRECT mode, which hands over decryptable credentials, is reserved for merchants certified to PCI DSS Level 1; the default path passes an encrypted, PSP-bound payload, never a raw PAN through the agent ([Google Pay UCP handler](https://developers.google.com/pay/api/universal-commerce-protocol/google-pay-payment-handler)). If the agent enters a PAN at a checkout, the agent runtime (headless browser or LLM context) handles cardholder data. That puts the app in PCI scope as a service provider and leaks the number into model context, against both the PAN constraint and the no-form-filling rule. Stripe says it bluntly in its own agent guide: "Don't hand your agent the card number" ([Stripe programmatic checkout](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/agents/programmatic-checkout)).

The workable shape is therefore a **token, not a card**: a credential minted by a licensed party, scoped to one merchant, one amount and a short expiry, and delivered to the merchant as a payment instrument inside a checkout protocol (UCP, ACP, MPP) or a network agentic token. Virtual cards remain useful only *behind* such a token. Stripe can, for example, fund an SPT from an Issuing card so that "an authorization must satisfy both your card controls and the SPT grant" ([Stripe programmatic checkout](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/agents/programmatic-checkout)). That path is private preview and framed as an agent paying "on behalf of your business", not for consumers.

## Stripe SPT is the only option that clears every constraint today

The three Stripe products differ sharply for an EU consumer app. **SPT** is documented as "available to agents, customers, and sellers in the US, Canada, and select European countries". The list covers AT, BE, BG, CH, CY, CZ, DE, DK, EE, ES, FI, FR, GB, GI, GR, HR, HU, IE, IT, LI, LT, LU, LV, MT, NL, NO, PL, PT, RO, SE, SI and SK ([Stripe SPT docs](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/concepts/shared-payment-tokens.md?agent-seller=agent)). The same rollout appears on Stripe's roadmap as **public preview, Q4 2026** ([Stripe roadmap](https://stripe.com/roadmap.md)). In the flow, the user enters a card in Stripe's Payment Element, the agent issues an SPT to the seller's Stripe profile, and the seller creates a PaymentIntent with it. The merchant's acquirer charges the user's own card, Link, Apple Pay, Google Pay, Klarna or Affirm. The agent platform touches only `pm_*` and `spt_*` identifiers and never receives funds ([Stripe SPT docs](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/concepts/shared-payment-tokens.md?agent-seller=agent)). The legal entry ticket is a Stripe account, a Stripe profile and acceptance of the "Agentic Commerce Agent Services Preview" terms. No licence requirement is mentioned. The API runs on a preview version (`2026-09-30.preview`), so terms and shapes will move.

SPT controls fit an engine that decides first and then enforces through the credential. The agent sets `seller_details[network_business_profile]`, `usage_limits[currency]`, `usage_limits[max_amount]` and `usage_limits[expires_at]`, and can "revoke the SPT at any time" ([Stripe SPT docs](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/concepts/shared-payment-tokens.md?agent-seller=agent)). The token is **amount-capped, not strictly single-use**: it "remains usable until it expires, you revoke it, or cumulative captured payments reach `usage_limits[max_amount]`" ([Stripe programmatic checkout](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/agents/programmatic-checkout)). Setting max_amount to the exact approved total and expiry to minutes gives merchant + amount + time scoping. The platform gets lifecycle webhooks (`requires_action`, `active`, `used`, `deactivated`) but **no real-time approve/decline hook** at authorisation. MCC restrictions are not documented.

The other two Stripe products fail for EU consumers. **Link Agent Wallet** is available to "US and Canadian customers" only, and link.com says "coming soon globally" with a waitlist ([Link Agent Wallet docs](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/agents/link-agent-wallet); [link.com/agents](https://link.com/agents)). **Issuing for agents** offers the strongest controls: an `issuing_authorization.request` webhook with a **2-second default timeout** before fallback to card rules, MCC lists, per-authorisation limits and `lifecycle_controls.cancel_after.payment_count` for single-use cards ([Stripe Issuing for agents](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/agents); [lifecycle controls](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/controls/lifecycle-controls)). In the EEA, though, Stripe documents only *commercial* issuing. The consumer card programme is **private preview**, and its documentation is entirely US (USD, SSN, Reg E) ([How Issuing works](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/how-issuing-works); [consumer prepaid debit](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/consumer-prepaid-debit-cards)). Issuing also needs a prefunded Issuing balance, so the platform fronts money, and the EEA agreement is titled "Commercial Card Program Agreement", with Stripe Technology Europe Ltd as the EMI ([Stripe agreement](https://stripe.com/fr/legal/issuing/commercial-card)). Single-use cards carry their own leak: network rules force Stripe to process force posts even after the card is cancelled ([lifecycle controls](https://docs.stripe.com/issuing/controls/lifecycle-controls)).

SPT's weakness is reach and SCA, not compliance. Only sellers with a Stripe account and profile that integrate SPT acceptance can redeem it, and no count of EU merchants doing so exists. Stripe's agentic selling for German businesses was announced in June 2026 as starting "later this year", and the research found no confirmation that it has shipped ([Stripe Tour Berlin](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-tour-berlin-2026)). SCA is not removed either: Stripe "might trigger 3D Secure" when the seller redeems the token. The SPT then moves to `requires_action`, and the platform must bring the user back to call `stripe.handleNextAction` ([Stripe SPT docs](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/concepts/shared-payment-tokens.md?agent-seller=agent)). An asynchronous agent therefore needs a way to pull the user back, possibly after the engine has already approved.

## EU issuers can issue the cards but cannot deliver them without a PAN

Among card-issuing-as-a-service providers, **Swan is the only one whose primary docs confirm every licensing and control piece**. Swan is an ACPR-licensed EMI passported across the EEA, and "individual account holders are issued consumer debit cards" ([Swan cards](https://docs.swan.io/topics/cards/); [Swan partnership](https://docs.swan.io/partnership/)). The client becomes a "Commercial Agent of Swan" and does not "have to complete a regulatory process" outside France, where it must register with ORIAS as an intermediary, and Spain, where Bank of Spain registration applies ([Swan partnership](https://docs.swan.io/partnership/); [Swan blog](https://www.swan.io/blog-posts/financial-compliance-for-platforms)). `SingleUseVirtual` cards exist in two variants. A one-off card has a mandatory spending limit and expires automatically after 30 days. A recurring card locks to the first merchant ID that authorises ([Swan virtual cards](https://docs.swan.io/topics/cards/virtual/)). Individual cards are capped at **€5,000 per rolling month**. The PAN is masked by default and unmasked only after cardholder consent, and "users can avoid 3-D Secure (3DS) consent when paying with SUV cards", because consent moves to card creation ([Swan cards](https://docs.swan.io/topics/cards/); [Swan virtual cards](https://docs.swan.io/topics/cards/virtual/)). Swan charges "a flat monthly fee plus fees per account and card"; the actual rates were not retrieved.

Swan still misses the brief in three places. First, **funding is a prefunded Swan account per user**, topped up by SEPA transfer. That keeps funds out of the app (they are e-money at Swan), but it is not "charge the user's own card". Second, the research found no partner-facing real-time authorisation webhook. The decision point is card creation, which preserves "no payment without a decision" but gives no second check at authorisation. Third and decisively, the merchant still needs the PAN. Without form filling and without a PAN-forwarding vault in the app's chain, a Swan card has nowhere to go. **Weavr** (Malta EMI since 22 July 2024) is the runner-up: "We hold the license; the regulatory responsibility sits with us", consumer KYC flows exist, and secure UI components keep card data "out of PCI scope" ([Weavr docs](https://docs.weavr.io/getting-started/why-weavr/); [The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/fintech/news/weavr-gains-european-e-money-licence)). Its spend controls and authorisation webhooks are unconfirmed, and a third-party review says consumer payments sit in a higher tier ([Fitgap](https://us.fitgap.com/products/022383/weavr)). Enfuce supports "both consumer and corporate programs" but sells BIN sponsorship to programme managers, not a self-serve API ([Enfuce](https://enfuce.com/docs/the-essentials/bin-sponsorship/)). Adyen, Marqeta, Nium and Wallester present as business-focused in Europe. The agent-card specialists (Crossmint, Rain) are US-only, and Crossmint's Agentic Cards API is limited to "eligible US-issued Visa credit and debit cardholders" ([Crossmint](https://crossmint.com/announcement/agentic-cards-api-launch-visa-basistheory)). **No EU provider found funds a consumer card per purchase from the user's existing card.**

## Google's protocols and the card networks set the EU future, not the present

**AP2 is now at v0.2 and under FIDO Alliance standardisation**, with Checkout Mandates and Payment Mandates, each open or closed, replacing v0.1's Intent/Cart/Payment ([ap2-protocol.org](https://ap2-protocol.org/)). A closed Checkout Mandate carries a merchant-signed checkout JWT, and the Payment Mandate's `transaction_id` is a hash of it, binding the payment to the priced cart ([arXiv AP2 analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.23858v1)). That is exactly the cart evidence the approval engine needs. **UCP** (Google and Shopify, January 2026) is the merchant-facing session protocol that carries AP2 mandates through pluggable payment handlers. Its endorsers include Adyen, Klarna, Mastercard, Visa and Zalando ([ucp.dev](https://ucp.dev/)). The catch is the credential provider. The Google Pay handler "enables users and merchants to use Google Pay as a credential provider on Google AI surfaces like Gemini and AI Mode" ([Google Pay UCP handler](https://developers.google.com/pay/api/universal-commerce-protocol/google-pay-payment-handler)). On those surfaces Google's agent is the caller and Google is the credential provider, so a third-party app is out of the loop. Google says UCP "can support any credential provider" ([American Banker](https://www.americanbanker.com/payments/news/google-shopify-unveil-new-agentic-protocol-for-retailers)), but no registration path for third-party credential providers is documented. UCP checkout on Google is live in the US and reportedly Canada. Only secondary guides cover Europe, and they describe a waitlist with no continental-EU date ([universalcommerceprotocol.eu](https://universalcommerceprotocol.eu/)).

Shopify gives the widest potential EU reach, with conflicting evidence. One blog says storefronts have advertised UCP at `/.well-known/ucp` since September 2026, including EU merchants; another says built-in agentic checkout shows only to US buyers ([presenc.ai](https://presenc.ai/research/shopify-ucp-self-serve-agent-profiles-2026); [kaspianfuad.com](https://kaspianfuad.com/blog/shopify-agentic-storefronts-explained/)). Shopify's September 2026 agent checkout tools run through browser-side WebMCP ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/28/shopify-opens-checkout-to-browser-based-ai-agents/)). These are structured tools rather than form filling, but they run in the buyer's browser and need a product ruling. ACP survives as an open spec, but OpenAI wound down standalone Instant Checkout in March 2026, and the research found no EU merchants live on ACP ([Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/what-it-means-that-the-leader-in-agentic-commerce-just-pulled-back/)).

The networks solve the PAN problem at the root, since agentic tokens replace the card number, but they are not open to an independent app. Mastercard says "all Mastercard issuers in Europe are enabled at a network level for Agent Pay" ([Mastercard Europe](https://www.mastercard.com/news/europe/en/perspectives/en/2026/europe-is-building-the-foundations-for-trusted-agentic-commerce/)). Santander's first live agent payment, though, "does not constitute a commercial rollout" and ran through an enabler, PayOS ([Mastercard press release](https://www.mastercard.com/news/europe/en/newsroom/press-releases/en/2026/santander-and-mastercard-complete-europe-s-first-live-end-to-end-payment-executed-by-an-ai-agent)). Visa reported live transactions with over 30 European issuers and four named merchants (lastminute.com, Frasers, Cleverbridge, BrickDepot), with merchants recognising agents through the Trusted Agent Protocol and Agent Directory ([Visa UK](https://www.visa.co.uk/about-visa/newsroom/press-releases.3457328.html)). On 18 November 2026 it extended "Agentic Ready" to 85+ European acceptance participants ([EuropaWire](https://news.europawire.eu/visa-brings-more-than-85-european-payment-acceptance-participants-into-ai-commerce-programme/eu-press-release/2026/11/18/16/50/04/188484/)). Neither network publishes a GA date or a self-serve path for non-bank agent developers.

## The legal position holds if the app never controls money

Under PSD2, which still governs in November 2026, the strongest characterisation of "our engine approves, the licensed provider executes" is the **technical-service-provider exclusion (Art. 3(j))**. It covers services that support payment services "without them entering at any time into possession of the funds to be transferred" ([PSD2 consolidated](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2015/2366/2025-01-17/eng/pdf)). The decisive test is control over funds, not control over whether a payment happens. Supervisors read possession broadly. BaFin counts a power of disposal "even if only for a short moment" ([PayTechLaw](https://paytechlaw.com/en/lets-distinguish-technical-service-provider-or-payment-initiation-service/)), and the EBA treats receiving and forwarding funds as money remittance ([EBA Q&A 2020_5216](https://www.eba.europa.eu/single-rule-book-qa/qna/view/publicId/2020_5216)). Five things would tip the app into licensing:

1. Charging the user's card into the app's own merchant account to load a card.
2. Initiating bank payments (PIS is explicitly outside Art. 3(j)).
3. Issuing instruments in its own name.
4. Holding stored balances.
5. Holding API keys that can spend a user's wallet without per-transaction user authority.

The fifth is the gray area that makes the engine's rules and the user-confirmed mandate legally meaningful, not just product features. The commercial-agent exclusion is a weak fallback, and the agreed PSR narrows it to agents with genuine authority to negotiate or conclude for one side ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/psd3-psr-what-the-eus-new-payments-rules-mean-for-your-business-102mrom)). PSD3/PSR was politically agreed in November 2025 and published as Council compromise texts in April 2026, but the research found **no confirmation of Official Journal publication by 19 November 2026**. Application lands around 2028 ([Avanto](https://avanto.team/insights/psd3-psr-status)).

Two PSR changes matter for the design. Creating or replacing a tokenised payment instrument, and changing spending limits, become explicit **SCA triggers** ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/psd3-psr-what-the-eus-new-payments-rules-mean-for-your-business-102mrom)). Minting a scoped token per purchase could then mean an SCA per purchase unless the issuer treats one mandate-level SCA as covering it. The engine's STEP_UP maps naturally to "ask the user for issuer SCA (a passkey) for this purchase". Delegating SCA to a third party also counts as outsourcing under the PSR, with contract, audit and liability duties ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/cedd39c6/psd3-and-psr-from-provisional-agreement-to-2026-readiness)), so the app can never be the SCA provider in its own right. No Commission or EBA guidance specific to agentic payments exists, and agent models "remain subject to PSD2 and the RTS requirements concerning SCA" ([Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/agentic-payments-new-challenge-europes-payments-ecosystem)). Under the AI Act, Article 50 transparency has applied since 2 August 2026 ([Stephenson Harwood](https://www.stephensonharwood.com/post/102nfqh/eu-ai-act-update-european-commission-adopts-guidelines-on-article-50-transparency-obligations/)). Any BNPL or creditworthiness feature would move the app into Annex III high-risk territory ([AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)).

### Recommendation and trade-offs

Model the payment step as a port whose output is a **scoped credential minted after APPROVE**: payee, exact amount in the cart's currency, expiry, revocation handle. Do not model it as "a card". Build the first adapter on **Stripe SPT**. It is the only option that meets all three hard constraints for EU consumers today. The price is preview terms, Stripe-only merchant reach, no authorisation-time veto and SCA redirects at redemption. Keep **network agentic tokens** (via an enabler or an issuer partnership) as the strategic second adapter. They solve PAN handling and SCA through passkeys, and Visa's November 2026 acceptance expansion shows reach growing. Treat **Swan SUV cards** as viable only if a PAN-free delivery path appears, such as push provisioning into a merchant or PSP vault. Otherwise they force either form filling or PAN custody. Swan's prefunded-account model also adds user KYC friction and a top-up step that SPT avoids. Reject Stripe consumer Issuing (not in the EEA) and Link Agent Wallet (US/CA) for now, and recheck both quarterly. The hardest trade-off is product, not legal: **without form filling, the payable EU merchant set in November 2026 is the small intersection of merchants exposing UCP/ACP/MPP sessions and accepting a token the app can mint.** No source quantifies that set. It needs measuring against the app's target shops before committing.

## Conclusion

The question assumed the virtual card was the unit of control. The evidence says it is the wrong unit. In the EU in late 2026, licensing and fund custody can be outsourced cleanly, but **card-number custody cannot be, as long as the merchant end still expects a card form**. Control therefore has to live in a scoped token that the merchant's protocol understands. The app's real dependency is not an issuer but merchant adoption of UCP-style checkout sessions and agentic tokens. That shifts the strategic risk from regulation, which looks manageable under Art. 3(j), to reach, which nobody has yet measured.

The AP2 v0.2 design also validates the engine's architecture. A closed Checkout Mandate bound to a merchant-signed cart hash is the same "priced cart evidence, then decision, then scoped credential" sequence the control layer already enforces. The PSR's coming SCA trigger on token creation will turn the engine's STEP_UP from a product courtesy into the moment the law requires.
